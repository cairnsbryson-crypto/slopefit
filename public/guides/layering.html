Buying guide

How to layer, without overthinking it

Most people buy a warmer jacket and wonder why they're still cold. Warmth isn't the jacket. It's the air you trap underneath it, and how well you get rid of sweat.

Layering has a reputation for being complicated, mostly because it's usually explained by people trying to sell you three things. It's actually simple, and most riders already do a version of it without calling it that.

Three layers, one job each

LayerIts one job
BaseMove sweat off your skin. Its job is keeping you dry, not warm.
MidTrap air. This is where your warmth actually comes from.
ShellKeep weather out and let vapour escape.

That's the whole system. If you're cold, the answer is almost never a thicker shell — it's a mid layer you're missing or a base layer that's gone damp.

Your mid layer is probably a sweater, and that's fine

Gear guides like to tell you to buy a technical fleece. In reality most people ride in a long-sleeve base and a sweater — no hood, because a hood bunches up under your jacket collar and gets in the way of a helmet — or sometimes a hoodie. That works. A sweater traps air, which is the whole job.

Where a proper fleece earns its money is that it keeps insulating when it's damp and dries fast afterwards. If you only ride a handful of days a season, the sweater you own is fine. If you're out every weekend, a fleece is the cheapest real upgrade in the system.

Dress so you're slightly cool starting out

This is the one rule worth actually following. Dress so you feel slightly cool standing at the bottom — by about your fifth run you want to be neither sweating nor so cold you have to call it a day.

If you're comfortable standing in the lift line, you're overdressed. You'll be sweating by the second run, that sweat will sit in your base layer, and then you'll freeze on the chair with nothing to do about it. Being a bit cool for the first five minutes is the trade, and it's a good one.

Cotton: it depends where you ride

Every guide on the internet tells you never to wear cotton. The honest version is that it depends on where you are and how long you're out.

At a local hill on a cold, dry day — a few laps, a break inside, home by dinner — a cotton hoodie is genuinely fine, and most people wear one. Cold snow doesn't soak in, and you're never far from a warm room.

It turns into a real problem when the day gets long or the snow gets wet. Somewhere like Whistler or the Alps, you're out for a full day, you're riding harder, and the snow is wet enough to soak through. Cotton holds the water it absorbs and keeps it against your skin, and a wet layer pulls heat out of you for hours. That's the situation the rule was written for. The same goes for the end of our own season — spring slush will find a cotton layer the way a February morning never does.

So: hoodie at the local hill, merino or synthetic for a big trip or a long day.

Down or synthetic

If you do buy a dedicated insulated mid layer, this is the only real choice in it.

Down is the warmest there is for its weight and packs down to nothing. It also stops working almost completely when it gets wet, and takes a long time to dry. Synthetic isn't as warm for the weight, but it keeps insulating when damp.

For resort riding, synthetic or fleece is the safer pick most days — you're sweating into it, and snow melts on you. Down makes more sense as something you pull on at the top of a hike, or on a genuinely cold, dry day.

This is the argument for a shell

An insulated jacket is a mid layer sewn permanently into a shell. That's fine in January and a problem in April, because you can't take it out.

A shell plus two midlayers covers the whole season — drop the mid on a warm day, add a thicker one when it's brutal. That's why our catalog runs about four shells to every insulated jacket, and why the quiz leans that way too.

Where the money goes

If you're spending, spend in this order: gloves, base layer, shell, mid layer.

Gloves first because cold hands end a day faster than anything else, and there's no layering your way out of them. Base layer second because it's cheap and it's doing the job everything else depends on. A $400 shell over a soaked cotton t-shirt will lose to a $150 shell over a decent base layer, every time.

If you're cold, check in this order: is your base layer damp, do you actually have a mid layer on, and are your extremities covered. Almost every "I need a warmer jacket" problem is one of those three, not the jacket.

The short version

Base to stay dry, mid for warmth, shell for weather. Dress slightly cool and let five runs sort it out. Wear the hoodie at your local hill, but take something proper on a big trip. And buy the shell, not the insulated jacket, if you're only buying one.
