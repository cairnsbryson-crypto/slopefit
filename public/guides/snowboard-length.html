Buying guide

What length snowboard do I actually need?

The short answer: your weight decides it, not your height.

That surprises people, because the advice everyone repeats is "stand it next to you and it should reach your chin." That rule is old, and it's wrong often enough to matter. A 5'10" rider who weighs 140 lb and a 5'10" rider who weighs 200 lb should not be on the same board, and the chin test can't tell them apart.

Weight decides it because a snowboard is a spring. You bend it to turn. Bend comes from the weight you put through it, so how much you weigh determines whether a given board flexes the way it was designed to. Too long for your weight and you can't bend it, so the board feels dead and turns like a plank. Too short and you overload it, so it washes out at speed and feels twitchy.

Then adjust for where you ride

Park: take off about 4 cm. Shorter boards spin easier and are less punishing when you land slightly off. Every centimetre you drop is less board to swing around.

Powder: add about 5 cm. Backcountry: add about 6. Length is float. A longer board keeps your nose up in deep snow instead of sinking, which is the difference between surfing and doing push-ups all day.

Moguls or ice: take off about 2 cm. Both reward quick edge-to-edge changes, and a shorter board changes edges faster.

All-mountain groomers: leave it alone. The chart is already built for you.

Then adjust for how you ride

Beginner: take off about 2 cm. A slightly short board is more forgiving while you're learning to turn, and easier to skid when you need to bail. You will grow out of it, and that's fine — that's what learning looks like.

Intermediate: no change.

Advanced: add about 2 cm. Expert: add about 3. More edge holds better at speed and stays calmer in chop. You've earned the extra effort it takes to bend it.

Worked example

You weigh 175 lb, you mostly ride park, and you'd call yourself intermediate.

160 cm (from the chart, at 175 lb) −4 cm (park) ±0 cm (intermediate) ─────── 156 cm

Go looking for a 155 or 156. Boards come in 2 cm steps, so land on whichever exists — that's a smaller difference than the one between two brands' idea of the same number.

Things the chart can't tell you

Board width matters more than most people realise. If you wear a US 11 or bigger, look for a wide model. You don't want your toes hanging over the edge — they catch in the snow when you're leaning into a turn, and it makes the board harder to carve. No amount of correct length fixes that. If you're a 7 or smaller, you get the opposite problem: a standard board is more width than you need underfoot.

Brands disagree with each other. One company's 156 rides longer than another's, because effective edge — the part actually touching the snow — varies with the shape. Treat the number as a starting point, not a spec.

A 2 cm difference is small. People agonise over 155 versus 157. Honestly, the gap between those two is far less than the gap between a stiff board and a soft one. If you're between sizes and undecided, go shorter if you're in the park and longer if you like going fast.

None of this sizes your bindings or boots. Get boots fitted in person by someone who knows what they're doing — an ill-fitting boot ruins more days than a slightly wrong board length ever will. And bindings must be mounted and adjusted by a qualified technician.
