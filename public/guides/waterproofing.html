10K, 20K, 30K — what do waterproof ratings actually mean?

It's the number everyone asks about, and most jackets never tell you what it is.

We keep a catalog of 132 jackets and pants, every one checked against the brand's own listing. Exactly 30 of them state a waterproof rating. The other 102 say nothing at all — not because they're bad, but because the number is a marketing choice, and plenty of good brands skip it. So this guide has to answer two questions: what the number means when you get one, and what to look at when you don't.

The number is a column of water

A 10K rating means the fabric held back a column of water 10,000 mm tall — ten metres — before it started seeping through. 20K is twenty metres. It's a lab test with a tube of water, not a weather forecast.

What it really tells you is how hard you can press wet snow into the fabric before it gives up. That's the thing that matters on a real day: sitting on a soaked chairlift, kneeling in slush to strap in, someone landing on you. Pressure plus water is what gets through, and the number is a measure of pressure.

There's a second number, and nobody talks about it

You'll often see it written 20K/20K. The first number is waterproofing. The second is breathability — roughly, how much sweat the fabric lets out over a day.

This is the one people skip, and it's why an expensive jacket can still leave you wet. If you run hot, hike to anything, or ride hard enough to sweat, a jacket that keeps water out but won't let vapour through just soaks you from the inside instead. You end up damp either way and blame the waterproofing.

How much do you actually need?

RatingWhat it handles
5K–10KCold, dry days and resort laps. Genuinely fine for most people most of the time.
15K–20KThe sweet spot. Wet snow, slush, sitting down, a full day out in weather.
20K+All-day storms, heavy coastal snow, and anything in the backcountry.

Most people overbuy. If you ride a few weekends a season at a local hill, 10K with decent construction will keep you dry, and the money is better spent on gloves — cold wet hands end a day faster than a damp shoulder ever will.

Spring is the test, not January

Here in Ontario, 10K is usually fine for the whole season — our winter snow is cold and dry, and cold snow mostly brushes off.

Spring is where it changes. Once it warms up the snow turns genuinely wet, and wet snow behaves completely differently: it sticks, it soaks in, and it sits on you while you ride instead of blowing off. A jacket that was fine all winter can start letting through in March and April. If you ride late into the season — or you're the one still out there in slush in April — that's the argument for 20K rather than 10K. Not the January cold snaps.

Same goes anywhere with a wet snowpack. The Pacific Northwest and coastal British Columbia get that kind of snow all winter, which is why the gear sold there skews higher.

Seams leak before fabric does

This is the part that matters more than the rating. A 10K jacket with taped seams will keep you drier than a 20K jacket without them.

Every seam is a line of needle holes through an otherwise waterproof fabric. Taping covers them from the inside. "Fully taped" means all of them; "critically taped" means the shoulders and hood and not much else. Water also finds zips, cuffs and the hem long before it soaks through a panel.

So if a brand publishes a big number but says nothing about seams, be suspicious. If a brand publishes no number but talks about fully taped seams and a storm flap over the zip, that tells you more.

When your jacket "stops being waterproof"

There's a coating on the outside called DWR — a surface treatment that makes water bead up and roll off. It is not the waterproofing. It wears off with use and washing, usually within a season or two.

When it goes, the face fabric soaks up water instead of shedding it. The jacket goes dark and heavy and feels clammy, and it stops breathing properly because the wet outer layer blocks vapour. This is called wetting out, and it gets mistaken for a failed jacket constantly. The membrane underneath is usually fine — the jacket needs washing with a technical cleaner and reproofing, not replacing.

Shell or insulated is the bigger decision

Our catalog runs about four shells to every insulated jacket, and that ratio reflects where the market has gone. A shell is just the waterproof outer layer; you add warmth underneath with a midlayer. An insulated jacket has the warmth built in.

A shell handles far more conditions, because you can add and remove layers as the day changes. An insulated jacket is simpler and warmer standing still, but on a mild spring day you can't take the insulation out, and you'll cook. If you're buying one jacket to cover a whole season, buy the shell.

One honest caveat on the numbers. There's no single enforced standard behind them, and brands test their own fabrics. A 20K from a brand that cares and a 20K from one that doesn't are not the same jacket. Construction, seams and fit tell you more than the figure on the swing tag.

The short version

For most people riding resort: 10K–20K with taped seams is plenty. Check the second number if you run hot or hike. Go 20K or higher if you ride wet snow — spring laps, or a coastal mountain. And if a jacket states no rating at all, that isn't a red flag; look at the seams, the zips and the cut instead.
